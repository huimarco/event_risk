    What this is

    A live map from geopolitical questions people bet money on to the investments those questions affect — so a risk desk can see which world events its portfolio is exposed to, which way each would push it, and how the odds are moving.

    Prediction markets such as Kalshi and Polymarket put a price on questions like "Will China invade Taiwan by the end of 2027?". A price of 9¢ for a contract that pays $1 if the answer is yes means traders collectively put the odds at about 9%. This project collects those odds for every geopolitical market, works out which markets are really the same question, connects each question to the countries, people and organisations it involves, and from there to stocks, ETFs and currencies.

    Why it matters

    • Geopolitical risk is usually described, not measured. Research notes say a conflict is "a risk"; they rarely say how likely it is today, whether that changed this week, or which holdings it touches. Prediction markets supply the missing number — a probability, updated continuously, with money behind it.
    • The odds are scattered and hard to use. The same question is listed on several venues in different wording; thousands of contracts expire, get renamed or vanish. Nobody links them to securities. This project does the cleaning, matching and linking.
    • The history can't be rebuilt later. Venues keep only part of their past prices and none of their past contract terms. Everything here is stored as it was known at each moment, so any past day can be replayed exactly — essential for testing whether the signal would have helped.

    It is built as risk-measurement infrastructure, not a trading signal: the consensus probability is the point — a shared, auditable number a risk committee can watch — and raw history is available to quants who want to build their own models.

    A worked example: selecting TSM shows "Will China invade Taiwan by December 31, 2027?" near the top — ▼ impact −1.46, odds 9.5%. The path reads: the question involves Taiwan as the target of an invasion (adverse for Taiwan) → TSM is exposed to Taiwan (its fabs are there) → so a YES pushes TSM down. A ceasefire question would show the opposite sign for a defense contractor like Lockheed Martin.

    Scale today: about 10,900 geopolitical markets, 10,700 propositions, 1,450 entities, 543 securities (the S&P 500 plus Asian chipmakers, country ETFs and currencies), and about 97,000 connections. Everything is produced by algorithms from public data — no AI models — except the analyst estimates marked below.

    Step 1 · Collect the markets

    Every 15 minutes the collector reads Kalshi's world and politics markets and Polymarket's geopolitics section: prices (bid, ask, last trade), open interest, and the full contract terms. Hourly history back to 2021 was backfilled once. A market is in scope if Polymarket files it under geopolitics, or — for Kalshi — if it involves a foreign state actor: a country other than the US, or a person or organisation belonging to one. "Will Trump visit China?" is in; "Will the Senate confirm…?" is out.

    Step 2 · Markets → propositions: is it the same bet?

    Questions that read alike are often different bets ("visits China before May 14" vs "on May 14"), so wording similarity is not used. Each market is parsed into a contract signature:

    FieldExample: "Will Trump visit Taiwan before Jan 1, 2027?"
    Actionvisit — one of ~50 event types (invade, strike, blockade, sanction, ceasefire, meet, leave office, appoint…)
    Actor → targetDonald Trump → Taiwan (which side of the verb matters)
    Time windowDec 2, 2025 – Dec 31, 2026, read from the resolution rules
    Threshold— (used for "tariff ≥ 25%", "more than 260 ships", "20–21 seats")
    Conditionsin person, phone calls count, becomes law, negated ("…agrees not to…") …

    Two markets become one proposition only if every field matches, and then real money gets the last word: if their prices differ by more than 10 points they are linked as near-equivalent instead, never averaged. Other safeguards: a negated question never matches the plain one; on the same venue the resolution rules are compared too, so two identical questions about different places are kept apart; each pair is judged once, at the moment the newer market was listed, so decisions never flip later.

    OutcomeMeaning
    mergedThe same bet as another market; one proposition, prices averaged (weighted by open interest).
    linkedA similar but different bet (other deadline, threshold or wording): connected, prices kept separate.
    unmatchedRead fine, but no other market asks exactly this — the usual case, since venues rarely list identical questions.
    unparsedThe event type isn't in the vocabulary yet, so it can't be compared (about one market in eight).

    The Markets tab lists every market by outcome, with its parsed signature and the reason each near miss was rejected. A person can override any decision.

    Step 3 · Propositions → entities: who and what is involved

    There is no hand-made list of countries or leaders; entities are rediscovered from the questions on every update.

    • Finding them. Every name-like phrase in a question ("Xi Jinping", "Strait of Hormuz", "ballistic missile") is looked up as a Wikipedia article title; the longest phrase that matches wins. A lone surname ("Xi") resolves to the one person already found with that name; a nationality word ("Chinese") to its country.
    • Knowing what they are. Wikidata, Wikipedia's structured sister project, says whether each is a person, country, organisation or concept, and which country it belongs to (Trump → United States), so "Trump makes a deal" and "the U.S. makes a deal" are recognised as the same side.
    • Their role in the question. From the contract signature: actor (China, in "China invades Taiwan"), target (Taiwan), party (both sides of a meeting), or concept (invasion, tariffs).
    • Connecting entities to each other. Wikidata facts (Xi Jinping — citizen of — China) plus a strength score from how often Wikipedia articles mention both:
    relatedness(A, B) = 1 − [ log max(|A|,|B|) − log |A∩B| ] / [ log W − log min(|A|,|B|) ]
    |A|, |B| = articles mentioning each · |A∩B| = articles mentioning both · W = all articles (~7.3M) — the Normalized Google Distance, computed on Wikipedia

    Example: Putin–Xi Jinping 0.66, China–Taiwan 0.56. Links need at least 10 articles mentioning both and a score of 0.25 or more. Concepts are kept only if specific (mentioned in under 1% of articles) and related to the question's actors — "tariff" yes, "company" no.

    Step 4 · Entities → securities

    Which securities depend on which entities — Taiwan → TSM, Strait of Hormuz → oil producers — is the one hand-written input: analyst estimates (0–1) from industry and headquarters rules, to be replaced by company revenue-by-country data and measured price reactions. A second small table says which industries gain when things get worse on a channel: defense contractors from conflict, oil producers from supply disruption. Everything else suffers with the entity it depends on.

    Step 5 · Direction and size of impact

    Each question's link to an entity records what a YES means for it. The event type sets a channel (conflict, trade, sanctions, instability, diplomacy), a severity (an invasion 1.0, a missile test 0.4, a meeting 0.2) and a direction by role: an invasion is adverse for the target and somewhat adverse for the invader; a ceasefire or trade deal is favourable for both; a negated or reversed question ("…ends", "…lifted") flips it. A security's exposure to a question adds up every path through the graph:

    path strength = severity × role weight × pass-through × exposure weight
    path sign = direction for the entity × how the security responds on that channel
    impact = Σ sign × strength over all paths · relevance = 1 − Π(1 − strength) · risk = |impact| × √(p (1 − p))
    pass-through = 1 for the entity itself, 0.5 × relatedness for a linked entity (one hop)

    Risk is highest for questions near 50% — a 99% event has little left to move. A portfolio's impact nets its holdings, so a position that loses in a conflict and one that gains offset each other.

    Step 6 · Checking it against reality

    The impact numbers are a structural prior — what the graph's logic implies — in graph units, not percentage returns. A backtest checks them against what actually happened: on days a question's odds jumped and stayed jumped, did linked securities move more than they usually do, in the predicted direction?

    Current result (October 2026, 62 securities with price history): not yet validated. The predicted direction was right about 50% of the time — chance. Linked securities moved about 12% more than usual on those days, but no more for strong links than weak ones, which suggests busy news days rather than the graph's links. The next run will cover all 543 securities. Turning this prior into measured event betas — "TSM falls x% per 10-point rise in invasion odds" — is the next stage.

    Numbers you will see

    ProbabilityThe latest price of the proposition's markets, averaged (weighted by open interest when available); only same-bet markets are averaged.
    24h changeProbability now minus 24 hours earlier, in points.
    RelevanceHow strongly a security is connected to a question, 0–1, combining every path.
    ImpactSigned: ▲ expected to rise if the event happens, ▼ to fall; "direction unknown" when the event type has no direction. A prior, not a measured beta.
    Risk|impact| × √(p(1−p)): how much the question can still move the security at today's odds.

    Time travel

    As of shows the graph and probabilities as they were at any past moment. Nothing is overwritten: every change is stored with when it became true and when the system learned it, so a past view uses only what was known then — no hindsight, which is what makes backtests honest.

    Data and updates

    MarketsKalshi and Polymarket public APIs — a snapshot every 15 minutes (in practice every few hours on the free scheduler), plus hourly history back to 2021
    GraphRebuilt only when markets are listed or their terms change, or the method changes; price moves never alter it. Each change is recorded, not overwritten
    EntitiesWikipedia and Wikidata, cached after the first lookup; refreshed monthly (facts) and yearly (relatedness)
    Security pricesDaily closes from Yahoo Finance — research use only, to be replaced by a licensed feed

    Current limits

    • Entity → security weights and channel responses are analyst estimates, not sourced figures.
    • Direction comes from a fixed table of event types; unparsed markets carry relevance without direction.
    • Securities and exposures were built around US–China, Taiwan and semiconductors, so many questions about other regions have few linked securities.
    • Security prices are daily and unlicensed; venue terms on redistributing raw quotes still need checking before raw data is sold.

    Using the API

    Everything in the explorer is available as JSON over HTTP. Base URL: BASE. Interactive reference with a "try it" button for every endpoint: /docs.

    Basics

    AuthenticationIf the server has API keys configured, send yours in the X-API-Key header. A missing or wrong key returns 401.
    Time travelEvery read accepts as_of (the world at that moment) and known_as_of (only what the graph had recorded by then), as ISO 8601 timestamps, e.g. 2026-06-01T00:00:00Z. Both default to now. Set both to the same time for a point-in-time, backtest-safe view.
    IDsPropositions prop:polymarket:665270 · entities wd:Q865 (Wikidata id; Taiwan) · securities sec:TSM · markets mkt:kalshi:KXTAIWANLVL4-29JAN01. Get them from the list endpoints.
    Probabilities0–1. prob is the blended price of a proposition's markets; change_24h is in probability units (0.05 = 5 points).
    Live onlyList endpoints return propositions priced in the last 7 days unless include_closed=true.
    Errors401 bad key · 404 id not found at that as_of / known_as_of · 422 invalid parameter.

    Quick start

    curl -H "X-API-Key: $KEY" "BASE/propositions?q=taiwan&sort=move&limit=5"
    import requests
api = requests.Session(); api.headers["X-API-Key"] = KEY      # omit if the server has no keys
top = api.get("BASE/propositions", params={"sort": "move", "limit": 10}).json()
for p in top["propositions"]:
    print(f'{p["prob"]:.1%}  {p["change_24h"] or 0:+.1%}  {p["name"]}')

    Propositions

    GET/propositions

    List propositions with probability and 24h change. Params: q (name search), entity (only those involving this entity id), sort = move (largest |24h change|, default) · prob · name, limit (≤ 500), include_closed.

    { "count": 151, "propositions": [
    { "id": "prop:polymarket:567621", "name": "Will China invade Taiwan by end of 2026?",
      "prob": 0.0215, "prob_24h_ago": 0.0205, "change_24h": 0.001,
      "close_time": "2027-01-01 04:59:00+00:00", "venues": ["polymarket"] } ] }
    GET/propositions/{id}

    One proposition: its venue markets (price, rules text), the entities it involves, and logical links (implies, mutex, near_equivalent) to other propositions, with the reason for each.

    GET/propositions/{id}/history

    Blended probability over time. Params: start, end (default: last 30 days), interval = 1h · 1d. Each point: {"t", "prob", "n_markets"}.

    Graph

    GET/graph/{node_id}

    The neighbourhood of any proposition, entity or security, for visualisation: nodes (id, type, name, depth, prob) and edges (src, dst, type, weight, provenance, note). Params: depth (1–3), fanout (strongest links kept per node).

    GET/entities

    All entities, optionally type = person · country · organisation_or_place · concept, with how many propositions involve each.

    GET/entities/{id}

    One entity: Wikidata description and Wikipedia link, propositions involving it, linked entities (with relation and strength), and securities exposed to it.

    Securities and portfolios

    GET/securities

    All securities (ticker, name, asset class, country, sector, index) and how many exposure links each has.

    GET/securities/{ticker}/exposures

    Propositions a security is exposed to, summed over every path through the graph: score (relevance 0–1), impact (signed; > 0 = expected to rise if the event happens; null when no path has a known direction), direction (up · down · unknown), risk (|impact| × √(p(1−p))) and the three strongest paths with their channel and sign. Params: sort = score (default) · impact · risk, min_score (default 0.1), limit, include_closed.

    { "security": { "id": "sec:TSM", "name": "Taiwan Semiconductor (ADR)" },
  "exposures": [ { "id": "prop:polymarket:567621", "name": "Will China invade Taiwan by December 31, 2027?",
                   "prob": 0.095, "score": 0.95, "impact": -1.46, "direction": "down", "risk": 0.43, "n_paths": 4,
                   "path": [ {"id": "prop:polymarket:567621"}, {"id": "wd:Q865", "name": "Taiwan"}, {"id": "sec:TSM"} ],
                   "paths": [ { "strength": 0.9, "sign": -1.0, "channel": "conflict", "role": "target", "nodes": [ … ] } ] } ] }
    POST/portfolio/scan

    Send holdings, get back the geopolitical propositions the portfolio is most exposed to: exposure = Σ weight × relevance score; impact = Σ weight × signed impact (holdings on opposite sides offset), with direction and risk. moved flags propositions whose probability moved at least move_threshold in 24h.

    curl -X POST "BASE/portfolio/scan" -H "Content-Type: application/json" -H "X-API-Key: $KEY" \
  -d '{"holdings": [{"ticker": "TSM", "weight": 0.10}, {"ticker": "NVDA", "weight": 0.08}],
       "move_threshold": 0.05, "limit": 10}'
    { "unknown_tickers": [], "count": 150, "propositions": [
    { "id": "prop:polymarket:567621", "name": "Will China invade Taiwan by end of 2026?", "prob": 0.0215,
      "change_24h": 0.001, "exposure": 0.177, "moved": false,
      "holdings": [ { "ticker": "TSM", "weight": 0.1, "score": 0.9, "via": ["Taiwan"] } ] } ] }

    Markets (match review)

    GET/markets

    Venue markets by how they fared in cross-venue matching: merged · linked · unmatched · unparsed. Params: status (repeatable; default unmatched + unparsed), q, venue, limit, include_closed. counts covers every status.

    GET/markets/{id}

    One market: status, parsed contract signature, parser issues, nearest rejected candidates with the reason for each, rules text and its proposition.
